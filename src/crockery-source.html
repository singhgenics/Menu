The look on the table

four materials, one story

The menu is Indo-Chinese cabin food, a dim sum steamer counter, Korean and Thai plates and a Goan craft-beer pub in one room. Each world gets its own material, so the table tells guests where a dish comes from. No white banquet china: that is what the Asian places nearby already use.

Cabin enamel

White enamel with a coloured rim

Cabin starters, fried bar bites, the Thai basil bowl and the lunch plates. The classic enamel plate: it doesn't shatter on the beer-garden floor, and chilli-red food looks loud on white. Pick an olive or rust rim.

Bamboo & porcelain

Steamers, soup spoons, chopstick rests

Everything from the steamer goes to the table in its basket, so lifting the lid is the photo moment. White porcelain spoons for every soup. Dim sum arrive in the basket they were steamed in.

Konkan stoneware

Hand-glazed bowls and plates

Bao, noodles, rice, soups, curries and desserts, in sand, olive, rust and charcoal glazes that sit well with limewash and wood. Buy from one Indian maker so pieces stack and reorder cleanly.

Clear glass

Six glass shapes cover the whole drinks menu: pint, 330, highball, rocks, coupe and nosing glass, plus shots and an Irish coffee glass. Kings and Budweiser bottles are poured into the pint and 330. No branded glassware; the beer is the colour.

Per cover

laying a place

What's on the table before the first plate arrives. Forks and spoons are on the table in a cutlery cup; many guests from the rest of India will reach for them first.

  1. Side plate, 7.5". Eat from it, and set a steamer basket on it when dim sum arrives.
  2. Rice bowl. Rice, noodles, curry and gravy are served into it from the sharing vessel; jasmine rice comes to the table in it.
  3. Dipping dish. Soy and chilli vinegar to start; swapped for black vinegar and ginger with soup dumplings, or kasundi with the prawns.
  4. Porcelain soup spoon. For soup, and for holding a soup dumpling while it cools.
  5. Chopsticks on a ceramic rest. Reusable, square-edged so they don't roll.
  6. Water glass, filled from a table carafe. Folded cotton napkin under the chopsticks.
  7. Per table: one condiment set (soy, green chillies in vinegar, chilli oil), a cutlery cup with forks and spoons, and coasters.

Before you order

buying notes

Where to look

  • Stoneware: Indian hotel-ware ceramic makers, or Khurja potteries for a custom glaze at volume. Get a sample of each glaze first.
  • Enamelware: hotel-supply wholesalers; check that it's lead-free and food-grade.
  • Steamers, porcelain spoons, chopstick rests: Chinese-kitchen suppliers (Mumbai's Crawford Market) or online restaurant-supply stores.
  • Glassware: one commercial range (tempered, stackable) so replacements match for years.

Test before a bulk order

  • Stack ten of each in the dish rack and glass rack you've planned. Thick stoneware and tall glasses eat space fast.
  • Run a piece of each through 20 dishwasher cycles. Cheap glaze dulls and enamel chips at the rim.
  • Put a full basket of dim sum on the 7.5" side plate. If it overhangs, step up to 8".
  • Pour a hot tom kha into the soup bowl and a hot green curry into the claypot. Both should be comfortable to hold on the side plate and the stand.

Bar and care

  • One bartender means one pair of hands: batch the syrups, rim salts and garnishes before opening, and keep two of each tool so one set is always clean.
  • Keep shot glasses and one rack of coupes in the freezer for chilled vodka and martinis.
  • Wash glass separately from greasy plates; a film of oil kills a beer's head.
  • Bamboo steamers: rinse only, no soap, dry upright. Buy steel-banded ones and budget 15% replacement a year.
  • Printed greaseproof paper under fried food keeps baskets clean and doubles as branding.
